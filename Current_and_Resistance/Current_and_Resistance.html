Current and Resistance

1 Introduction

1.1 Definitions

The electric current in a wire is defined as

I=dtdQ​

where dQ is the total amount of charge that passes through a cross–section of the wire in a differential amount of time, dt.

The SI units of I are Coulombs per second, C/s, which is defined to be an Ampere (A).

If q is the charge (in Coulombs) of each flowing charge, n is their number per volume (“number density”), vd​ their average speed along the wire (called the “drift speed”), then

I=n∣q∣vd​A, where A is the cross–sectional area of the wire. Caution: The A here is itallicized whereas the abbreviation of an Ampere, A is not itallicized.

The average current density is defined as

J=AI​, which can be written as J=n∣q∣vd​.

1.2 Ohm’s Law

If an electric field exists in a wire (by, for example, connecting its ends to a battery), the charges will accelerate until they collide with another particle and decelerate (collisions resist the flow). The net result will be current – a flow of charges with an average drift speed. Experimentally, it has been shown that in many materials, the electric field is proportional to the current density:

E=ρJ

where the value of the proportionality constant ρ, called resistivity, depends on the material. This is one version of Ohm’s law.

For a wire of length L and constant cross–sectional area A, Ohm’s law can also be written as V=(ρL/A)I. If we define resistance as R=ρL/A, then we have another relationship that is also referred to as Ohm’s law:

V=IR.

In this form, the interpretation is that the voltage between the ends of a wire is proportional to the current in the wire, with the proportionality constant of R.

2.1 Definitions and Ohm’s Law

A 9–volt power source is connected to a wire of length 10 meters with a circular cross–section and radius of 0.01 meters. The wire has a resistivity of 10−8 Ω⋅m. The number density of flowing charges is 1028/m3. Assume Ohm’s law applies and the charges that flow are electrons.

  1. What is the resistance (with units) of the wire?

    Answer: R=AρL​=π(0.01 m)2(10−8 Ω⋅m)(10 m)​=π10−3​ Ω. When solving circuit problems, the resistors involved typically have a much larger resistance than this, which is why we neglect the resistance of the wires.

  2. What is the current (with units) in the wire?

    Answer: I=π10−3​ Ω9 v​=9,000π C/s=9,000π A. This is a huge current. If you look at the back of an electronic device, you will see a rating on the order of 1 A. Household circuit breakers are set to open (“trip”) at approximately 15 A. Most power sources cannot supply current at this rate; even if the power source could supply this current, the amount of heat created would lead to a fire or melting of the wire.

  3. How much charge (in Coulombs) flows past a cross–section of the wire per second?

    Answer: 9,000π C

    Many student are confused by this question, perhaps because it is simple. If current measures the amount of charge that flows past a cross section per unit time, the amount of charge that flows past that cross section in a time of 1 s is the current x 1 s. Caution: The abbreviation of Coulomb is C (not itallicized). Later, you will work with capacitance, which we denote with C (itallicized) … and the unit of capacitance is the Farad (F).

  4. How many electrons flow past a cross–section of the wire per second? (The charge on an electron is −1.6⋅10−19 C.)

    Have you ever encountered a number on the order of 1023 before?

    Answer:

    9,000π C⋅1.6⋅10−19 C1 electron​≈1.8⋅1023 electrons

    Avogadro’s number, 6.02214076×1023, is the approximate number of atoms found in 12 grams of pure carbon-12. A mol is used to describe exactly 6.02214076⋅1023 items (such as atoms, molecules, ions, or electrons).
